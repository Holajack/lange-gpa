## 1. Phase identity & goals

- **Name:** Phase 4 — **Deep Personal Relationships** *(with a few people — impacting all relationships)*. Its central special-growth activity type gives it its alternate label: the **Deep Life Sharing** phase.
- **Position in the programme:** the **giant middle one-third** of the 1,500-hour Six-Phase Programme. Phases 1–3 ("beginner phases") total ~500 hours with **nurturers**; Phase 4 = **500 hours** (programme hours ~500–1,000); Phase 5 = 500 more hours; Phase 6 is ongoing and "lasts for years." Thomson's diagram: `Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3 | Phase 4 | Phase 5` = `500 hours | 500 hours | 500 hours`.
- **Ideal duration:** **four to six months**, "during which the growing participator concentrates his life (by whatever means) on this growing participation."
- **Character:** "This is the **make-or-break phase**, or perhaps the **sink-or-swim phase**. This is the phase when life among host people needs to take off and go very deep. (In Phase 5, it broadens out.)"
- **Core purpose:** stop being taught and start *sharing life*. The special helper changes from trained **nurturer** to untrained **mentor**. The GP learns the host world deeply **by learning individual host lives deeply** (Rule 4), via three ethnography-inspired activities: Life Story, Walk-of-Life Conversations, Detailed Observation.
- **Conceptual purpose — "the great cross-over":** Phase 4 is "pivotal… It is the phase of the great cross-over, going from living life among host people largely as a **they story**, to **living the host story with host people**." It is also the phase to escape the expat **"home-away-from-home archipelago"** and start living many weekly hours in genuine host contexts (work, leisure, recreation, domestic, community, faith, general social life).
- **Motto (title page):** "Don't learn the language! Rather, discover a new world, as it is known and shared by the people among whom you are living."
- **What "done" looks like:**
  - ~500 hours of special-growth activities logged (e.g., 300h Life Story + 100h Walk-of-Life + 100h Observe-and-Describe).
  - A few **genuinely deep** host relationships (shared joys and hurts, "real laughter… real tears") plus many meaningful ones — *"In Phase 4, additional relationships are mandatory."*
  - A large personal **Listening Library** (10–20+ hours of clarified recordings per main storyteller).
  - Listening vocabulary substantially past the ~4,000-word entry level, on pace toward the programme's 10,000-word goal.
  - Comfortable comprehension of extended natural explanations (expository speech) from multiple voices; able to retell moderately complex stories, summarize mentors, describe observed scenes in exhaustive detail.
  - Life has measurably shifted into host contexts; ready for Phase 5 (Native-to-Native Discourses, "doing things with words," style/register).

**Relationship arc across phases (Thomson's own table):**
| Phase | Relationships |
|---|---|
| 1 | Only rich growth relationship is the paid nurturer — "a pretty limited relationship!" |
| 2 | Nurturer relationship more meaningful; scattered shallow relationships possible |
| 3 | Nurturer relationship "deep indeed"; scattered other relationships becoming more meaningful |
| **4** | **"A few relationships can become deep. Many relationships can be quite meaningful."** |
| 5 | "Considerable freedom in forming meaningful and deep relationships." |

## 2. The instructions — how sessions are structured

Phase 4 has **no fixed lesson template or clock-based session plan**. Sessions are natural recorded conversations structured by **recursive activity cycles**, not by timed segments. The guide's Part 1 opens: "let's just plough into the activities!… There are three major activities that can easily occupy a growing participator for all of the 500 hours of Phase 4, but they can be also supplemented by additional activities, mostly borrowed from earlier phases."

### The canonical Life-Story cycle (quoted verbatim from the guide's summary box)
> **Steps in Life-story conversations**
> Step 1. Ask the initial whole-life question as, "Please tell me the story of your life." (Similar to a grand-tour question) RECORD.
> Step 2. Clarify. (Remember, this is a conversation to gain a clear understanding of this particular recording.) Record the Clarification Process.
> Step 3. Word Recordings (Record the whole discussion of new words.)
> Step 4. Identify possible expansions.
> Step 5. Choose a point to expand.
> Step 6. Ask further questions in order to do the expansions. (Record the expansions)
> Step 7. Repeat steps 2-6 (Cycle back as many times as you do expansions—maybe expansions of expansions of expansions
> Step 8. Retell (in a spirit of "Let me see if I understood your story.")
> Step 9: Add to the Listening Library all of the recordings in which the host person is talking.

### The canonical Walk-of-Life cycle (verbatim)
> Step 1. Ask the initial walk-of-life question such as, "What do you do as a taxi driver in a typical shift." (grand-tour question) RECORD.
> Step 2. Clarify. … Record the Clarification Process.
> Step 3. Word Recordings (Record the whole discussion of new words.)
> Step 4. Identify possible topics to expand: potential grand tours, mini tours, lists-of-life etc.
> Step 5. Choose a point to expand.
> Step 6. Ask further questions in order to do the expansions. (Record the expansions)
> Step 7. Repeat steps 2-6 …
> Step 8. Retell (in a spirit of "Let me see if I understood you.")
> Step 9: Add to the Listening Library all of the recordings in which the host person is talking.

### The Detailed Observation cycle
1. **Identify** social situations (take a walk, list places with actors + activities); choose one.
2. **Visit and take notes** — head notes and/or jottings (shorthand, diagrams, coded positions).
3. **Describe to your mentor** relying on the notes — every detail, "do not go easy on yourself"; ask what the *mentor* thinks it all means; **triangulate** important points with 2–3 other hosts.
4. **Record the mentor's summary and commentary**, deliberately including all new word-log words.

### Time distribution (the guide's actual planning instruction)
> "Try the three major activities right at the beginning of Phase 4, and then set personal goals as to how much time you will spend on each. As one example, you might set a goal of **300 hours of the Life Story Activity with five different host people, 100 hours of Walk-of-Life Conversations with representatives of five walks of life, and 100 hours of Observing and Describing**. You could structure this in many other ways, and it will be good if you can discuss it with a **language learning advisor**."

And: "It is fully possible to spend the entire five hundred hours just doing the Life Story Activity with a handful of host people. Limiting yourself in that manner would not be a great calamity, as that activity has proven to be **the most fruitful one** in many respects."

### The monitoring rhythm (vocabulary throttle)
> "We recommend that for every hour of special activities … you have strong encounters with an average of **eight or more new vocabulary items**. … if over the course of **four days**, you have added significantly less than eight words per hour of special activities, you should **set aside time on the fifth day** for activities which will expose you to many more new words" (hole-finding, unused Phase 2 picture stories, Phase 3 shared-story activities).

### Session-level texture
- Initial life-story tellings run **2 minutes to 10–20+ minutes**.
- During expansions, "your **fifteen second question may bring a five minute answer**" — the GP's voice barely appears on the recordings.
- Solo re-listening: "**At least once or twice more, the same day or within a few days**."
- Forms discipline: "Using this chart for a while will help you to get into the pattern of following the steps and sub-steps … **until they become second nature. When it has become second nature to you, then you will be ready for the alternative approach**" (the Two-Recorder Technique). Same instruction for the Grand Tour Form: "First use the form until it becomes second nature … rather than going quickly to the two-recorder approach."

## 3. What the NURTURER does (now called the MENTOR)

**The role changes name and nature in Phase 4.** From the introduction: "In Phase 3 the GPs need to branch out much more in their social network… the special host people who help them grow are called by a term that reflects this difference: **mentors**. … In Phase 4 (Deep Personal Relationships) and Phase 5 (Widening Understanding) **the mentors do not need training, as the communication is natural**, but they [GPs] still need people who will interact with them at a level they can understand and participate in."

Key properties and behaviors:
- **No training needed** — natural communication. But mentors instinctively **simplify**: "The people adjust their speech because of our limitations" (this is why Phase 4 runs vocabulary-light, and also why the recordings stay re-listenable for years — "they were geared toward you at a particular stage of your development").
- **Paid, for structured work:** for Walk-of-Life conversations, "As this involves extended conversations on more than one occasion, and making sound recordings of the conversations, **you should insist on paying the person a reasonable amount per hour based on local standards**." The first life-story mentor is very often a former nurturer "who has already been doing 'language learning' activities with you, and therefore, who is accustomed to recordings."
- **What the mentor does in sessions:**
  - Tells their life story / typical-day account at whatever length, in whatever direction they choose (Rule 1: "there are no rules governing your mentor!").
  - Pauses on demand during clarification and explains: unknown words ("wilderness"), unknown concepts (brief explanation of "camping"), idioms the GP misreads ("became really close to," "kept to myself"), missing cultural background (the 12-week California school break), or simply repeats more clearly when pronunciation was the problem.
  - Gives **brief** explanations during clarifying, **full** explanations during expansions (the warning is explicit: clarification is *not* the step to explore the whole topic of camping).
  - Answers grand-tour, specific, guided, task-related, mini-tour, experience, structural, and contrast questions; asks "Anything I missed?" and keeps going when the GP drills further.
  - Helps **fill and name lists of life** ("What are these examples of?" — and if no fixed cover term exists, coins one they're comfortable with).
  - In Detailed Observation: listens to the GP's exhaustive scene description, then **re-describes the scene as they understood it, with their own interpretations, recorded**, working in all the new word-log words.
  - In Record Yourself for Feedback: judges each sentence — "Is that something that I or another host person might have said, or is it clearly different from how host people would talk?" — and supplies the native version.
  - Discusses each word-log entry **in the host language** for vocabulary recordings.
  - In live talk generally: "host people who help you to formulate your sentences… **PAY ATTENTION, pay lots of attention, to the ways they help you and correct you**." Thomson's picture of natural nurturing: "the native helping them to find the word they are groping for, sometimes guiding them gently in the way they are forming their sentences, sometimes rewording what they themselves just said in order to make it simpler or clearer."
- **How the mentor's role changes across the phase:** early on the mentor mostly monologues (primary recordings) and clarifies; as the two-recorder style takes over, clarify/expand becomes one flowing conversation; late in the phase mentors are conversation partners in group life and feedback-givers on the GP's own output (feedback activity, journal feedback).
- **Consent & dignity around recording:** "Promise that no one but you will hear the recordings, unless the speaker gives permission… Promise that if the speaker decides that they want something they said erased, you will immediately and permanently erase it. **Keep your promises.**" Onboarding tactic: do a couple of days *without* recording, then point out how much is being lost and ask to try recording once.
- **Confidentiality is at professional level:** the relationship involves "trust comparable to that enjoyed by a **physician, lawyer, or priest**" (Crane & Angrosino, citing Kluckhohn). Non-personal anecdotes may be retold anonymously ("My neighbour had such an interesting experience…"); a grief like a miscarriage must never be retold identifiably. "Err far on the side of caution."
- **Expected by-product:** "**Expect a strong bond to form** with anyone with whom you do extensive life story conversations." Atkinson: life-story listening "can be one of the most enjoyable and rewarding interpersonal experiences ever"; benefits to the *teller* include clearer perspective, stronger self-esteem, joy, purging of burdens, community. "In life story conversations, **the first priority is to be a good listener—to let our mentor have the joy of being truly understood.**"
- **A separate figure — the language learning advisor** — helps plan hour allocation and the home-world/host-world life audit.

**Mentor/GP don'ts (consolidated):** never steer the storyteller "back on track"; never ask about unmentioned topics (Rule 3); don't push personal/painful topics in the first days (a parent's death mentioned in the initial story — don't expand it on day one); don't offer your own speculations about observed scenes before hearing the mentor's; don't treat one host's off-the-cuff explanation as final (triangulate); never violate recording promises or anonymity.

## 4. What the PARTICIPANT (GP) does

**Response mode: the GP is a full conversational participant now** — no pointing, acting, or TPR responses remain. The modes are: *ask broad questions → listen intensely → record → clarify → log words → plan expansions → re-listen → retell/summarize → observe & describe → self-record for feedback → write.*

In-session:
- Asks **one broad open question** ("Please tell me the story of your life"; "Tell me what you do in a typical day") and then stays almost silent, showing genuine interest via host-culture listening behaviors deliberately observed since Phase 3 (eye contact? facial expressions? repeated "yes" word? "Mhm," "Really!," "Imagine that!").
- Tells the mentor, sincerely, how interested they are and "remind[s] them often of how ignorant you are of life in their world."
- Operates the recorder(s); stops the recording at every comprehension gap and works out with the mentor *what kind* of problem it is.
- Maintains the **running word log** (~4,000 words at entry), noting which recording and where each word occurred; counts new words per recording on the tracking forms.
- Builds **vocabulary recordings** (see §9).
- Harvests expansion candidates *only from things mentioned or alluded to*; fills in the **Stories to Record Form / Grand Tour Form**.
- **Retells/summarizes** back: "try to summarise to your mentor what he/she has told you thus far. This will stretch your talking ability… and will also help your mentor to feel understood and appreciated."

Between sessions (homework / self-directed):
- **Solo listening** to the raw recording before the next session (optional but popular): identify non-understood parts, note expandable bits, draft questions.
- **Re-listen to clarified recordings** once or twice within days, attending to logged words "as they parade before you again in their original contexts"; archive to the Listening Library.
- **Observation fieldwork**: walks to list social situations; visits with jottings/head notes; immediate write-up ("The longer you wait, the less your head notes will survive"); optional covert phone dictation or video.
- **Writing**: begin a **journal addressed to a host nurturer** and get feedback (if written ≈ spoken language).
- **Deliberate talking volume** (the "Ways to Talk a Lot" menu): describe detailed observations; periodically retell mentors' content; retell non-confidential anonymized stories to "friends, co-workers, taxi drivers, your barber!"; tell **your own life story** to mentors and key incidents to various people; tell an **epic story in small instalments** to several people; join host groups requiring oral contribution (faith discussion groups, special-interest groups, "a group of people that regularly gather in the same teashop"); maintain an active visiting social life; and exploit the universal multiplier — "Any strategy that involves telling the same story… to several different host people on different occasions will be profitable. You will notice great improvement **from the first telling to the second, and on to the fourth or fifth retelling**."
- **Life restructuring**: audit work/leisure/recreation/domestic/community/faith/social domains for ways to let host-world life "compete" with the home-away-from-home world; discuss with the language learning advisor.
- **Attitude work**: "Decide that there is no place to go but forward… Get in there. Slog on. Slog, slog, slog." And the anchor principle: "**no matter how badly it is going, participation in relationships is growth.**"

## 5. WHAT they are learning

**Vocabulary (with numbers):**
- Entry state: "you will have some acquaintance with perhaps **4,000 words** by the beginning of Phase 4" (the word log "by the end of Phase 3 may contain 4,000 words already").
- Programme goal: "**10,000 words in our listening vocabulary**" — "you are nowhere near our goal… You mustn't slow the pace."
- Operational rate: **≥ 8 strong new-vocabulary encounters per special-activity hour**, monitored over rolling 4-day windows with a day-5 infusion rule.
- Content skew: Phase 4's natural conversations are vocabulary-light because mentors simplify and topics are everyday — hence the supplementary vocab machinery.

**Grammar / structures:**
- The programme's built-in progression: "here-and-now grammar → simple story grammar → **complex story grammar (foreground, background, flashbacks)** → **grammar needed for explanations (talking in general ways, in abstract ways, or talking hypothetically)**." Phase 4 lives at the last two levels — expository language is the phase's comprehension frontier.
- Grammar defined for the GP: "the glue that connects words together" — word order ("he doesn't" vs "doesn't he"), word grouping ("happy horse riders"), small function words, word-form variation (run/ran, cat/cats).
- Developmental readiness doctrine: "you may be ready for some particular aspect of grammar **when you start noticing it a lot, and even trying to use it in your own speech**" (e.g., "the X who is Y," "He likes to X," "Someone helps him to X") — at which point run **input flooding / output flooding** from the Phase 3 guide.
- Honest limits: some grammar ("the/a" in English) "will puzzle you for a long time, perhaps indefinitely"; the only ultimate cure is "hearing host people talk, while understanding what they are saying, **for thousands of hours**."

**Discourse skills:**
- Understanding long natural narratives and explanations from **multiple speakers with different voices and pronunciations**.
- Producing: summaries, retellings of moderately complex stories, exhaustive scene descriptions, one's own life story, serial epic storytelling, and — later in the phase — "extended contributions that flow smoothly" in host group discussions.
- Phase 5 preview kept in view: society-wide **Discourses** (the capital-D ongoing "big conversation" about hockey, computers, the stock market), "doing things with words" (requests, apologies, commitments), and register/style — in Phase 4 "we can be asking about **themes, people, places and events that are universally known to host people**."

**Cultural knowledge:**
- The host world *from within*, on host terms: individual life histories; walks of life (roles: rickshaw driver, nurse, shopkeeper, rancher); **cultural domains / lists of life** and their cover terms; **key events** (weddings, births, deaths, holidays, religious events, plus random major events like house fires) and cyclically recurring events; values and assumptions revealed by expansion and by mentors' interpretations of observed scenes; what hosts consider worth mentioning vs unremarkable (the woman who omitted being raised by relatives ages 6–15 — the omission itself was culturally significant).
- The **languaculture lesson**: story-building pieces don't translate (bathrobe ≠ khalat; grocery shop ≠ azyk tulik dukeni; tea ≠ shai; bowl ≠ kece); "the story building pieces (experience building pieces) probably never match up across languacultures."

**Social expressions / interaction:**
- Host **listening behaviors** (back-channels, eye contact, silence patterns) — deliberately deployed.
- How hosts correct, rephrase, and scaffold a struggling speaker — and how to absorb it.
- Group-conversation etiquette: "You will want to fit in and not be overly disruptive to the flow of group life."

## 6. HOW they are learning it — every named activity, with mechanics

### A. The Life Story Activity (Activity 1 — "the most fruitful")
Mechanics (Steps 1–7 plus the 9-step cycle in §2):
1. **Initial recording** — "Please tell me the story of your life." First versions run 2 to 20+ minutes. No steering: a Central Asian man opened with the Leningrad blockade, thousands of miles away — his point was that he was born at the height of WWII. "They are creating the track for the life they wish to share." Worked example: **George's story** (born Los Angeles 1947; Canada at nine in 1957; ranch summers 1960–65; the winters back in California where "they would never know the real me").
2. **Solo listen** (optional): mark gaps, note expandables, draft questions.
3. **Clarify the recording** — replay together, pause at every gap. The guide types the problems with nine worked examples from George's excerpt: *unknown word* (wilderness, fishing, socialised), *unknown concept* (camping — brief explanation only; ranchers), *idiom misread* (became really close to; kept to myself), *polysemy* (spent — money vs time), *missing cultural background* (the three-month school break), *pronunciation*. All new words → word log. **"The goal of clarifying the recording is to become so familiar with it that whenever you listen to it again, you largely understand it."**
4. **Spot expansion opportunities** — Rule 3: only from mentioned/alluded material. Good: "Have you been told anything about the time when you were born?"; "Tell me all about those two months in 1957"; "Tell me about those ranchers." Bad (italicized anti-examples in the guide): "What happened in 1958?", "Did you graduate from High School?", and "**Did you have any girlfriends?**" — "an extreme violation of the rule… pure curiosity." Interests surface naturally: mention of the wife → ask about the wife → the wedding emerges → ask for the wedding story → ask how it differed from / resembled other weddings.
5. **Record the expansions** — two kinds: **(a) a new story** filling in the original (George's birth: the father overseas writing daily letters promising a daughter, the dresses, the spicy Mexican food, the Hollywood cowboy roping his pregnant mother with a lariat), or **(b) a full explanation of a new concept** ("Tell me what people do when they are camping"). Expansions explode: the 90-second excerpt yields ~10 expandables; **10-minute story → ~90 minutes of expansions → potentially 9 hours of further expansions; one storyteller easily yields 10–20 hours of recordings**. "Soon you will learn to be very selective."
6. **Re-listen** 1–2+ times within days; add to Listening Library. (Gate check: "If you find that you do not fairly readily reach the point where… you can understand the story well then you are probably not ready for Phase 4 activities.")
7. **Summarise** to the mentor.
Tracking: the **Stories to Record, Parts to Expand, Topics to Explore** form — columns: *audio recorded ✓ | clarified ✓ | number of new words | checked for new topic ideas ✓ | listened to again ✓ | listened to later ✓*; topics numbered and cross-derived (George's line 8 "Father's experience in the Army" spawned from expansion 2).

### B. Vocabulary Recordings (sub-technique)
Two formats: **(a) words-in-context sandwich** — copy sentences containing each new word into a new audio file, word before and after: "Spent. I spent my early childhood in California. Spent." / "Ranchers. We became really close to many of the ranchers. Ranchers." ("If you remember the basic context, hearing it should jog your memory.") **(b) word-discussion recording** — record GP + mentor going down the word-log list "briefly discussing each word (in the host language, of course)" — Thomson finds this "somewhat more interesting… for later listening."

### C. The Two-Recorder Technique (advanced alternative)
Once the step-by-step procedure is second nature, intermingle the steps. Recorder A plays the **primary recording** (the mentor's original story); recorder B captures the whole clarify-and-expand conversation (the **secondary recording**) — the primary ends up scattered through the secondary, interleaved with the GP's questions ("what's a lariat?") and the mentor's answers. If the mentor "starts to take off telling an animated story," stop interrupting and treat it as new primary material; clarifying *that* later produces a **tertiary recording** ("I've never gone beyond that, making a quaternary recording, but it is conceivable"). "It all just flows together… these secondary recordings… make fascinating listening material in later months and years."

### D. Walk-of-Life Conversations (Activity 2 — ethnographic conversations à la James Spradley)
Present-time daily life in a societal **role** (vs. Life Story's diachronic life). Thomson's own case: a **rickshaw driver in Pakistan** ("I was a constant user of rickshaws"). Recruit from professions impinging on your life (nurse if doing medical work; shopkeeper since you shop); do **several walks of life**; **pay** local hourly rates. Mechanics:
- **Typical grand-tour question** first ("typical" = asking for a typical example: a typical day, shift, or process — e.g., building a house). Emphasize to the teller: (1) what's commonplace to them is unknown to you; (2) everything is interesting, give lots of detail.
- **Clarify** as always; word log; then harvest into the **Grand Tour Form** (adds a "checked for lists of life" column).
- **More grand tours** from the answer: typical year; typical day other seasons; fencing; bringing hay down from the high meadow; layout of the ranch.
- **Mini-tour questions** — brief activities in fine detail: making the fire in the cookstove; putting on the coffee; coffee break; eating breakfast/dinner/supper; breaking holes in the creek; getting the team and hitching them up; throwing hay into the rack; descriptions of the barn and the shop. The full Jack transcript is the model: kindling with pre-made shavings, chimney/damper management, "When she's really roarin', you close down the chimney a bit," ending "Anything I missed?" — GP: "well, what exactly do you do when you light a piece of kindling?" ("Don't get hung up debating whether a particular question is a grand tour question or a mini-tour question.")
- **Spradley's full descriptive-question typology** (guide pp. 86–88 citation): *typical grand tour; **specific** grand tour* (what Jack did last Wednesday, an actual day); *guided* grand tour (Jack shows you every place in the barn in person); *task-related* grand tour (Jack harnesses a horse narrating each step); *mini-tour*; *experience questions* ("Have you had any really interesting experiences while feeding the cattle?" — these "reflect expectations, assumptions and the kinds of problems that can arise when things don't go as expected"). Plus **structural questions** ("What are some other evening activities?") and **contrast questions** ("What is the difference between a day in haying season and a day in the early fall?").

### E. Lists of Life / Cultural Domains (sub-technique of Walk-of-Life)
A **cultural domain** = a set of same-type items that "exists 'out there' either in the language, in the culture or in reality" (not personal preference lists — "the set of colours that a given respondent likes to wear is not… a cultural domain"). From Jack's day: *things that need workin' on; different places where there is hay; times to get up in the morning; different buildings on the ranch; Ellen's tasks; places on the ranch; breaks during the day; times of the year (work-wise); conditions of the creek; after-supper activities.* Fill by read-back-and-ask: GP: "…Is there anything else you might do?" Jack: "Oh, maybe a bit of crib with Ellen. Or just kick back." GP: "Read, listen to the radio, play crib, kick back. Anything else?" Jack: "Yeah read, or look at the catalogue; maybe play a bit of solitaire." Then extend across informants (ask Ellen; read the list to other ranchers — "if 'winter evening activities' would turn out to be an extensive cultural domain… you have discovered an important aspect of ranch life"). Find the **host cover term**: "What are these examples of?" — or have the mentor coin one. **Spradley's nine domain relationships** (1979, p. 111): strict inclusion (X is a kind of Y); spatial (X is a place in / part of Y); cause-effect; rationale (X is a reason for doing Y); location-of-action (X is a place for doing Y); function (X is used for Y); means-end (X is a way to do Y); sequence (X is a step/stage in Y); attribution (X is a characteristic of Y). Thomson's weighting: rewarding, but "I don't find this activity any more important than asking grand tour questions, mini-tour questions, and other types of descriptive questions."

### F. Detailed Observation Activity (Activity 3 — Observe and Describe)
A **social situation** = place + activities + actors (Spradley 1980; e.g., a small food shop with sub-places like the cash register, actors like customers/shopkeepers, activities like gathering food and checking out). Mechanics: list situations on a walk → choose one → observe with **head notes** (deliberate memorizing — must be written up ASAP) and preferably **jottings** (invented shorthand; diagram the location; code positions/actors — bus seat "3RA" = 3rd row right aisle, "D" = driver, "C" = conductor). Record *everything*: "wall paper in the cafe, half-full trash can about ten inches high to the right of the door, stain on waiter's apron, aromas… who talks first, who talks how much." Crucially: "**Don't think about what you might or might not be easily able to describe in the host language.**" Worked example: the ~300-word 6-a.m. Central-Asian petrol-station sweeper description (broom construction — "thin sticks bound together… maybe thirty centimetres wide—the whole thing over a metre long"; arc of each sweep; dust clouds; red-brick vs sloping-asphalt zones; sweepings pushed into the street). **Modern variant** (credited to Tawnya Gililland): dictate into a phone's voice recorder — "it will appear that s/he is simply having a phone conversation!" — or use video. Then: describe to the mentor in full ("do not go easy on yourself"), ask the *mentor's* interpretations (Who is the man with the broom? What is behind his sweeping? Who is the sweeping important to and why? Significance of the hour?), **triangulate** anything important with 2–3 more hosts (worked example: several hosts infer "labourer, not supervisor" from his dress — confirmed; "sweeps early to avoid the heat" — rejected by others, who just felt a business should be clean when it opens). Finally the mentor records a summary + commentary including all new words. **Special case — one-time events**: recognize rare important events as they happen (an automobile accident, a fight, "a memorial meal following a fixed period after a death"), take heavy head notes → jottings → Steps 3–4.

### G. Hole-Finding (supplementary — vocabulary)
"You try to talk about something that is difficult for you, in order to find holes in your ability, such as unknown words." Concrete versions: narrate **a Tom & Jerry cartoon or a silent movie (Charlie Chaplin, Mr. Bean — 'both easy to purchase in many countries')** describing "absolutely everything that happens"; open a **busy picture** or children's **busy book** (Brocklehurst et al. 2003, *The Usborne Big Book of Things to Spot*) "to any page and try to describe what you see." Describing Detailed Observations is itself hole-finding. Trigger: the 4-day / 8-words-per-hour rule (§2, §5).

### H. Recycled Phase 2 activity (supplementary — vocabulary)
"Use a **wordless picture story that you have not used before**, and attempt to tell the story to your mentor. This is a good time to use **Anno's Journey** (Anno, 1977)."

### I. Recycled Phase 3 activities (supplementary — vocabulary + grammar)
"Any of the Phase three activities are likely to bring a steady influx of new vocabulary. This includes clarifying world stories, or host stories you know from translation, or building new scripts of life." Also **Input Flooding and Output Flooding** "may continue to be helpful… The key is that you use these activities to raise your awareness of aspects of grammar that you find yourself struggling with" (procedures in the Phase 3 guide, *"Getting going in Phase 3: 250 Hours of Shared-Story Activities"*). After such sessions: "make a recording recapping the activity and including all of the new vocabulary."

### J. Record Yourself for Feedback (supplementary — nativeness audit)
Tell a story/topic to a mentor **recording yourself**; replay **pausing after every sentence**; the mentor asks "Is that something that I or another host person might have said…?" If not, log in a three-column chart: **What I said | How a host person might have said it | Optional statement of the nature of the problem**. Guide's examples: "He blew her nose." → "She blew her nose." → "'He' refers to males." / "My friend were there." → "My friend was there." → "he was, she was, they were, we were, you were!" (third column may be left empty). Flagged patterns become "a source of ideas for future input flooding or output flooding activities."

### K. Writing Activities (supplementary — first appearance of writing)
"Phase 4 is also a good Phase in which to **begin writing**, if the written language doesn't differ radically from the spoken language. You can begin writing a **journal addressed to a host nurturer**, and get feedback on that as well." Caveat: awareness-raising is secondary — "more important than that is to keep interacting with host people who help you to formulate your sentences."

### L. Special-Growth Talking Activities: Ways to Talk a Lot (supplementary — output volume)
Eight-item menu (full list in §4). Rationale: "Of the three main Phase 4 activities… two of them—Life Stories and Walk-of-Life Conversations—involve you in a lot of listening and only a little talking. However, in order for your own talking to become smooth and easy, you need to do a lot of talking yourself."

### M. Topic Discovery Discipline (cross-cutting method)
Let the host culture dictate topics (contra "classical anthropologists" with checklists like "mate selection" and "bodily adornments, cosmetics, ornaments and apparel"). Three legitimate topic sources: (1) anything already mentioned (the expansion engine); (2) **key events** (Fetterman) — random major events (house fire), life-cycle events (weddings, births, deaths), religious events, holidays, cyclically recurring daily/weekly/monthly/yearly events; (3) anything personally observed or puzzling (Saturday-morning crowds — "Where might they be going?"; "times when people seem unexplainably upset with us"; a pedestrian struck by a car and the crowd's reactions). **Sensitizing concepts** (your interests: values, the supernatural, exchanges of possessions or services, alienation, reconciliation, major life events) may be pursued — "when they arise naturally in our mentor's discussions." Conversational sensitivity example: if the mentor is twelve years older than her youngest sister, ask what she remembers of the birth — "which we wouldn't ask if she is only three years older."

## 7. Stages/sub-phases & the grower's state

**Phase 4 has no formal lettered sub-phases** (no 4A/4B analogous to Phase 1A/1B). But the guide encodes a clear internal progression:

1. **Entry gate / readiness test:** "if you can carry out the activities smoothly, you are ready. If you have done something at least similar in spirit to the idealised Six-Phase Program for five hundred hours, then it is time to try Phase 4 activities." If the Life Story Activity is exceedingly hard the first day or two, **press on for several days** (perfectionists "must be encouraged to go on at some point! You may need the sink-or-swim spirit!"). **Fallback rule:** "if after a week or two it doesn't get any easier, then you should spend more time with Phase 2 and 3 activities combined with increasing informal social interaction. Then try returning to Phase 4 Activities after a month or two." Chief cause of unreadiness: failing to develop the necessary relationships during Phase 3. (Also: entering after fewer than 250 Phase-3 hours is fine if the activities go smoothly; and learners who never used Phases 1–3 can enter directly if able — though even coaching newcomers through Phase 1 teaches surprisingly much.)
2. **Early Phase 4 — form-driven, discrete steps:** use the tracking forms and the separated Steps 1–7 "until they become second nature"; avoid the most personal expansion topics in the first days with each mentor; possibly a few days without recording while earning recording permission.
3. **Mid Phase — fluid two-recorder style:** steps intermingle; primary/secondary/tertiary layering; selectivity about which expansions to pursue (the explosion forces it).
4. **Late Phase — lifestyle shift:** "later in Phase 4 you may be able to make extended contributions that flow smoothly with the contributions of the others" in host groups; life should be visibly moving from special-growth participation toward **lifestyle participation** (the Phase 6 destination), with real weekly hours in host contexts.

**The grower's state (Thomson's ability-by-phase recap plus Phase 4 description):**
| Phase | State |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Understood isolated statements and commands when you can see what the nurturer is talking about, and struggled to talk in highly constrained ways." |
| 2 | "Started to understand sequences of statements that made up simple stories… talking in isolated statements about what you could see, and starting to talk flexibly." |
| 3 | "Started to understand rich, complicated stories when the content was familiar in advance… started to understand explanations ('expository' language). In your talking, you started to tell simple stories." |
| **4** | Understands most of a naturally-told (mentor-simplified) life story with clarification; handles lots of explanations; **summarises what they've been told; retells moderately complex stories; shares their own story "transformed into a new story within the host world"; occasionally explains views on complex matters; engages with values and emotional issues** as depth develops. |

**What they still cannot do / the texture of the struggle** (the "Tough Times" section, at length): "You will have months of difficult struggling ahead. Understanding host people requires tremendous concentration… you're aware that you are missing a lot, and hesitant to stop and ask for clarification as often as you would like. … you launch off into a sentence, and realise that you don't know how to finish the sentence you started… You are often embarrassed that you used a wrong word (wanting to say *tomato* you said *jealous*…). You may talk, talk, talk, sensing that you aren't getting much right!… no pain, no gain… I sympathise with you, but you just gotta do it!" Native-to-native speech among insiders remains largely out of reach — that wall is Phase 5's business.

## 8. What they need to be LOOKING AT

Phase 4 is **ear- and world-centric, not picture-card-centric**. The visual/physical inventory:

- **The recorder(s)** — the central artifact. "A notebook and a recorder" essentially define the phase's kit. Two devices for the Two-Recorder Technique; a phone (voice memo, video) as covert observation tool.
- **The two paper tracking forms**: the *Stories to Record, Parts to Expand, Topics to Explore* form (Life Story) and the *Grand Tour Form* (Walk-of-Life, adding the "checked for lists of life" column) — the GP looks at these constantly to plan and log.
- **The running word log** (a notebook/register, ~4,000 entries at entry) with per-recording location references.
- **The real host world itself** — social situations observed live: shops, buses, petrol stations, ceremonies, kitchens; the GP's own **jottings, shorthand, and scene diagrams** (coded seats/actors like 3RA/D/C).
- **In-person guided/task-related grand tours**: literally standing in the barn while Jack describes every place in it; watching Jack harness a horse as he narrates — seeing while hearing.
- **Hole-finding visuals**: Tom & Jerry cartoons; silent films (Charlie Chaplin, Mr. Bean); **busy pictures** ("example to the right" — the guide itself carries one) and children's busy books (*The Usborne Big Book of Things to Spot*, Brocklehurst, Dogerty, Milbourne & Gower 2003).
- **Wordless picture books not yet used** — explicitly **Anno's Journey** (Anno 1977): "This is a good time to use Anno's Journey."
- **Written text — now allowed and encouraged (first phase where writing formally begins):** the GP *writes* a journal addressed to a host nurturer and reads the feedback; also writes down new words (even in no-recording cultures: "It should almost always be fine to write down (or have your mentor write down) new words"). The Record-Yourself-for-Feedback three-column chart is a written artifact. Precondition: "if the written language doesn't differ radically from the spoken language."
- **Reference tables**: Spradley's nine domain-relationship types as a lookup card; the ethnographic question typology.
- Notably **absent**: no Lexicarry, no TPR objects, no picture-card decks, no dictionary drills — those are Phase 1–2 furniture. Phase 2/3 materials reappear only as day-5 vocabulary infusions.

## 9. How much RECORDING they need to do

Recording is the **backbone of Phase 4** — more central here than in any other phase. Everything the mentor says in a session is meant to end up on audio.

**What gets recorded (the full inventory):**
1. The **initial life story** (primary recording) — 2 to 10–20+ minutes.
2. The **entire clarification process** (Step 2 of the 9-step cycle: "Record the Clarification Process") — the secondary recording under the two-recorder scheme.
3. **Word recordings** — "Record the whole discussion of new words" — in either sandwich format (word–sentence–word) or discussion format.
4. **Every expansion** ("Record the expansions"), recursively — expansions of expansions of expansions.
5. **Tertiary recordings** — clarifications of animated stories that erupted during secondary recordings (quaternary "conceivable" but never needed by Thomson).
6. Walk-of-Life: the initial grand-tour answer, its clarification, all follow-ups (mini-tours, lists-of-life fill-ins, experience/structural/contrast answers).
7. Detailed Observation: the **mentor's recorded re-description and commentary** of the observed scene, deliberately containing all the new word-log words; plus optionally the GP's own covert **phone dictation** (and even video) of the scene itself.
8. **Record Yourself for Feedback**: the GP's own extended speech, replayed sentence-by-sentence.
9. Recap recordings after hole-finding / recycled Phase 2–3 activities, "including all of the new vocabulary."

**Volume numbers the guide gives:**
- A 90-second story excerpt yields ~10 expandable bits; a **10-minute story → ~90 minutes of expansions → potentially 9 hours of further expansions**.
- "In a very short time you develop **ten or twenty hours of story and explanation recorded from a single storyteller**."
- Sample plan: 300h of Life Story across **5 storytellers** — i.e., the Listening Library grows by dozens of hours of clarified audio over the phase.
- Fifteen-second questions → five-minute answers: the recordings are overwhelmingly mentor-voice (Step 9 archives "all of the recordings **in which the host person is talking**").

**Re-listening prescriptions:**
- After clarifying any recording: "it is good to listen to it **a few more times in the days following**."
- After clarifying stories/expansions: "**At least once or twice more, the same day or within a few days**, you ought to listen to them again," attending to logged words in context.
- Long horizon: "**Years later you will be able to go back and benefit from listening again**… Because they were geared toward you at a particular stage of your development, they will soon become relatively easy and inherently interesting to listen to."
- The form's last two columns institutionalize this: *listened to again* / *listened to later*.

**Consent, fallback, and ethics (fully specified in the guide):**
- Promises: only you will hear it; permission required to share; **immediate permanent erasure on request**; keep the promises.
- Onboarding: first mentor is usually recording-accustomed; or do 2 days unrecorded, then demonstrate the loss and ask to try once.
- **No-recording cultures** (women's voices not to be heard by strangers; government-eavesdropping histories): write notes from memory immediately after; summarize back next meeting; still record (or write) **new words + illustrative sentences**, which "should almost always be fine."
- Informal conversations (retelling anonymized topics to taxi drivers, barbers) are *not* recorded: "Most of us are not going to be carrying a recording device around to record these natural conversations."

## 10. Milestones & phase-specific terminology

### Milestones / progress markers
- **The clarification-comprehension gate** (doubling as the readiness test): can elicit, clarify, and then *largely understand on re-listen* a mentor's recorded story "after a moderate amount of clarifying."
- 10–20+ hours of clarified recordings per main storyteller; a Listening Library spanning multiple mentors.
- Vocabulary: sustained **≥8 new words/hour**; word log growing from ~4,000 toward the 10,000 listening-vocabulary goal.
- Comprehends expository explanations and complex stories from **multiple different voices/pronunciations**.
- Talking: summarizes mentors' stories; retells the same story to several people with marked improvement by the **4th–5th telling**; tells own life story; describes minutely observed scenes exhaustively; by late phase, makes **extended contributions flowing smoothly** in host group discussions.
- Relationship metric (the phase's true metric): "**A few relationships can become deep. Many relationships can be quite meaningful**" — with real laughter and real tears; "In Phase 4, additional relationships are mandatory."
- Cultural method mastery: harvests expansions only from mentioned material; spots and fills lists of life; triangulates interpretations across 2–3 hosts.
- Lifestyle metric: measurable weekly hours living in host contexts across work/leisure/domestic/community/faith/social domains ("the archipelago needs to be broken down a bit").
- Writing begun (journal with feedback) where written ≈ spoken.
- Positioned for Phase 5: aware of Discourses, "doing things with words," and register — already asking about universally-known themes, people, places, events.

### Glossary of this guide's terminology
- **Growing Participator (GP)** — the learner, reframed as growing in participation in a host world, not "learning a language."
- **Six-Phase Programme / 1,500 hours** — Phases 1–3 ≈ 500h (beginner, with nurturers); Phases 4 & 5 = 500h each (non-beginner, with mentors); Phase 6 ongoing.
- **Special-growth participation (activities/times/sessions)** — concentrated paid sessions with special host people, needed because everyday life offers only scattered growth opportunities (service encounters and bureaucratic encounters aren't nurturing).
- **Nurturer** (Phases 1–3, trained) vs **Mentor** (Phases 4–5, untrained — "the communication is natural") — the load-bearing terminological shift of this guide.
- **Language learning advisor** — the third figure; helps plan hour allocation and life-domain choices.
- **Languaculture** (Agar 1994) — the flow of life with its lived and spoken stories and conversations; language and culture as one.
- **Symbol = form + concept** (Vygotsky, via Wertsch) — humans experience life *through means*; word-forms plus concepts are the master toolkit; "The impact of language on the world that people know is colossal."
- **Story-building pieces** — the words, objects, actions, scripts a languaculture builds lived and told stories from. Canonical example: **bathrobe vs khalat** (the identical Chinese-manufactured article becomes a different piece of life in Canada vs Kazakhstan); also kece ≠ bowl, shai ≠ tea, azyk tulik dukeni ≠ grocery shop.
- **They stories** — stories built about host people using home-languaculture pieces ("I saw a Kazakh woman in a bathrobe drinking tea from a small bowl") — "radically different from the story the host person is living." Expat old-timers "will be quick to orient newcomers with appropriate they stories!"
- **The host Story** — the shared life host people are living; Phase 4 is "**the great cross-over**" from they-stories toward living it.
- **Home world / host world; home-away-from-home world; the archipelago** — the transplanted expat world of "home-life islands in the great sea of host people — island hopping from home-world activity to home-world activity"; two North Americans in a host context "flood out" the host context.
- **Clarifying (a recording)** — replaying with a host, pausing at every gap, until the recording is largely understood on re-listen (practiced since Phase 2).
- **Word log** — running vocabulary register (~4,000 entries entering Phase 4), with recording-location pointers.
- **Listening Library** — cumulative archive of clarified, re-listenable recordings.
- **Primary / secondary / tertiary (/quaternary) recording** — the Two-Recorder Technique's layers.
- **Expansion** — follow-up material of two kinds: a new story growing from the story, or a full explanation of a new concept; expansions recursively "**explode**."
- **Grand tour question** (Spradley) — broad descriptive question over a long span/large place; subtypes **typical, specific, guided, task-related**.
- **Mini-tour question** — same for a brief activity in fine detail.
- **Experience question** — "Have you had any really interesting experiences while X?"
- **Structural question / contrast question** — fill a domain / probe differences within it.
- **Lists of life / cultural domains** (Spradley) — culturally shared same-type sets with **cover terms**; nine relationship types (kind-of, spatial, cause-effect, rationale, location-of-action, function, means-end, sequence, attribution).
- **Social situation vs cultural scene** (Spradley) — place+activities+actors described "thinly" vs with cultural meaning attached.
- **Thick vs thin description** (Geertz, via Ryle) — "wink" vs "rapid contraction of the right eyelids"; the guide's twist: "**We cannot see meaninglessly**" — even thin descriptions use home concepts, so ask hosts what *they* see.
- **Head notes vs jottings** (Emerson, Fretz & Shaw 1995) — memorized observations (decay fast; write up ASAP) vs shorthand field notes with diagrams and codes.
- **Triangulation** (Fetterman 1989) — confirming an interpretation with two or three host people before trusting it.
- **Key events** (Fetterman) — revealing events to stay alert for: house fires, weddings, births, deaths, religious events, holidays, cyclic events.
- **Key actor / informant** (Fetterman) — the ethnography term for the person one comes to know deeply; the GP analogue is the mentor.
- **Sensitizing concepts** — the GP's own interest areas (values, the supernatural, exchanges, alienation, reconciliation), pursued only when they arise naturally.
- **Hole-finding** — talking about something hard to expose gaps for the mentor to fill.
- **Input flooding / output flooding** — Phase 3 grammar-awareness techniques recycled for stubborn patterns.
- **Record Yourself for Feedback** — sentence-by-sentence native-plausibility audit of one's own recorded speech.
- **Discourse vs discourses** — the capital-D society-wide "big conversation" on a topic (hockey, computers, the stock market) vs individual conversations within it; full treatment is Phase 5, previewed here.
- **"Doing things with words"** — requests, acceptances, declines, apologies, forgiveness, commitments — flagged as Phase 5 material.
- **Person-centred ethnography** (Wolcott 1999) — knowing a people by knowing persons; the GP's approach "is also person-centred."
- **Deep Life Sharing vs anthropology** — GPs are cross-cultural and aim at holism (2 of Wolcott's 3 marks) but skip the comparative/theoretical apparatus: "Growing participators are much more fully concerned than anthropologists with functioning **from within** the insider's perspective, not primarily building outsiders' theories regarding the insider's perspective!"
- **Lifestyle participation** — the destination: life itself provides rich growth beyond special sessions (fully arriving in Phase 6).
- **Sink-or-swim / make-or-break** — Thomson's epithets for Phase 4 itself.
- **The Four Rules of deep-life conversations**: (1) no rules govern the storyteller; (2) be genuinely interested and act accordingly; (3) ask only about what was mentioned or alluded to; (4) grow in understanding of host life by understanding individual host lives.

### Key references cited by the guide
Agar 1994 (*Language Shock*); Anno 1977 (*Anno's Journey*); Atkinson 1998 (*The Life Story Interview*); Brocklehurst et al. 2003 (*Usborne Big Book of Things to Spot*); Crane & Angrosino 1992; Emerson, Fretz & Shaw 1995 (*Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes*); Fetterman 1989 (*Ethnography: Step-by-Step*); Geertz 1973, 1984; Grindal & Salamone 1995 (*Bridges to Humanity*); Roberts et al. 2001 (*Language Learners as Ethnographers*); Spradley 1979 (*The Ethnographic Interview*), 1980 (*Participant Observation*); Wertsch 1985 (Vygotsky); Wolcott 1999.

## App vs. source-guide deltas

The `phases.ts` entry (id 4, slug `deep-relationships`, lines 393–503) captures the spirit and the major activity names well, but diverges from the Thomson guide in these specifics:

### Contradictions / errors
1. **Two-Recorder Technique is mischaracterized.** App (`p4-tworecorder`): "**Your telling** on one recorder, your mentor's richer version wrapped around it on a second — compared side by side." In the guide, the primary recording is the **mentor's** story, not the GP's telling, and the secondary recording is the clarify-and-expand *conversation about* it — nothing is "compared side by side." The app's `how` text is closer ("Recorder one holds the original story…") but the description sentence inverts who is talking, and the `kind: "speaking"` tag is wrong — it's a listening/clarifying workflow. It is also an *alternative workflow* for Activities 1–2 (adopted only after the step-by-step forms are second nature), not a separate parallel activity.
2. **"Nurturer" used where the guide mandates "mentor."** `p4-lifestory` says "exploring your **nurturer's** life history" and part `4-life` says "Your **nurturer's** life, chapter by chapter." The guide's central Phase 4 shift is precisely that these people are **mentors** who "do not need training." (Only the journal-writing activity in the guide mentions addressing "a host nurturer.")
3. **"Guided heart-to-heart interviews — childhood, school, work, love, loss"** (`p4-lifestory` how) contradicts Rules 1 & 3: the GP asks ONE whole-life question and may only expand what the mentor already mentioned; there is no chapter agenda (childhood/school/love) — "Did you have any girlfriends?" is the guide's canonical anti-example of exactly this kind of pre-set chapter list.
4. **Hour allocation differs from the guide's example.** App parts: Life Stories **~200h**, Walks of Life **~200h** (walk-of-life + observe), Your Voice **~100h**. Guide's sample plan: **300h Life Story (5 people) / 100h Walk-of-Life (5 walks) / 100h Observe-and-Describe**, with the note that even 500h of pure Life Story "would not be a great calamity." The app's invented "Your Voice ~100h" block has no counterpart — feedback/hole-finding/two-recorder are supplementary or alternative techniques, not a 100-hour part; and the app demotes the guide's most-fruitful activity from 300h to 200h.
5. **Vocab target framing.** App: "+3,000 words → ~7,000 total." Guide: entry ≈ **4,000** words, programme goal **10,000 listening words**, and a pace floor of **8+ new words/hour** (which over 500h would be ≥4,000 words, i.e., → ~8,000). The app's 7,000 is an interpolation the guide never states; the 8/hour rule and the 4-day/day-5 monitoring mechanic are omitted entirely.
6. **Walk-of-Life topic list drift.** App: "weddings, markets, farming, raising children." The guide's Walk-of-Life is about **roles/professions** (rickshaw driver, nurse, shopkeeper, rancher, taxi driver) at the present time; weddings etc. emerge through Life-Story *expansions* and key-event alertness, not as walk-of-life topics.
7. **Milestone "Host people treat you as becoming 'one of us'"** — quoted as if from the guide but not in it; the guide's Phase 4 relationship claim is "a few relationships can become deep; many can be quite meaningful," and full insider participation is framed as Phase 5/6 territory.

### Omissions (prescribed by the guide, absent from the app entry)
8. **The Four Rules** of life-story conversations (no steering; genuine interest; mentioned-only expansions; lives→life) — the behavioral heart of the phase.
9. **The recursive expansion engine and its numbers** — two expansion types (new story vs full concept explanation), the 10-min → 90-min → 9-hour explosion, 10–20 hours per storyteller, selectivity.
10. **The two tracking forms** (Stories to Record Form; Grand Tour Form) with their checklist columns — the guide's core session-management artifacts and an obvious app feature.
11. **The clarification problem taxonomy** (unknown word / unknown concept / idiom / polysemy / cultural background / pronunciation) with the nine George worked examples.
12. **Spradley's question typology** (typical/specific/guided/task-related grand tours, mini-tours, experience, structural, contrast questions) and the **nine cultural-domain relationships** — the app mentions "Lists of Life" but none of the mechanics (read-back fill-in, cover-term elicitation, cross-informant extension).
13. **Triangulation** (2–3 hosts confirm an interpretation) and **head notes vs jottings**, scene diagrams/coding, the covert phone-dictation variant, and the **one-time events** protocol for Observe & Describe.
14. **Recording consent & confidentiality protocol** — the promises (sole listener, erase on request), the no-recording-culture fallback, physician/lawyer/priest-grade trust, anonymized retelling rules. Given the app records audio, this is arguably the most important omission.
15. **Payment of mentors** at fair local hourly rates (mandatory for walk-of-life sessions).
16. **The 8-new-words/hour floor with 4-day monitoring and day-5 vocabulary-infusion rule**, and the recycled **Phase 2/3 activities** (unused wordless picture story — *Anno's Journey*; world/host stories; scripts of life; **input/output flooding**) as the prescribed remedies for Problems 1 and 2.
17. **Writing activities** — the journal addressed to a host helper with feedback; Phase 4 is where writing formally begins, and the app has no writing activity.
18. **Special-Growth Talking Activities menu** (Problem 3) — retell to many people (1st→4th/5th-telling improvement), own life story, epic story in instalments, joining host groups, active visiting life. The app's only nods are `p4-observe`/`p4-feedback`.
19. **Readiness test and fallback loop** — press on several days; if no easier after 1–2 weeks, return to Phase 2/3 + informal interaction for a month or two.
20. **The Listening Library and re-listen prescriptions** (1–2 re-listens within days; years-later value) — the app's `p4-vocab` mentions "a permanent listening library" in passing but no scheduling.
21. **Retell/Summarise step** (Step 7 / Step 8 of the cycles — "Let me see if I understood your story") — absent as a distinct beat.
22. **The whole Part 2 conceptual layer**: they-stories / the great cross-over / languaculture & story-building pieces (bathrobe vs khalat), home-vs-host-world competition ("the archipelago"), tough-times encouragement ("participation is growth… slog, slog, slog"), topic-discovery discipline (key events, sensitizing concepts), the language learning advisor, Phase-5 preview (Discourses, doing-things-with-words), and Deep Life Sharing vs anthropology.
23. **Session `minutes` values** (45/15/40/30/25/20/30) are app inventions — the guide gives no per-activity minute prescriptions; its only timing figures are 2–20+ min initial tellings, 15-sec questions → 5-min answers, 500 total hours, and 4–6 months.

- `hours: 500`, `startHour: 500` — matches the guide's structure exactly.
- The five core activity names (Life Story, Vocabulary Recordings word–sentence–word, Walk-of-Life, Observe and Describe, Record Yourself for Feedback, Hole-Finding) are all genuine guide activities with essentially correct one-line mechanics (`p4-vocab`'s "word–sentence–word, the sentence drawn straight from today's conversation" is exactly the guide's sandwich format, though the guide's *preferred* alternative — the recorded word-discussion — is omitted).
